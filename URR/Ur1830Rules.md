## **1 Introduction**

### **1.1 Ur, 1830 BC**

From the temple, one can see far over the lands of Ur. The king points to teams of men digging near the river. "Look, my son. The men of Kishi are digging a new canal. We shall dispatch some workers to shovel water into it. Make sure they water our lands well." But I do not listen to the old king. I am contemplating whether to lead my men in founding a new nation closer to the river's source, or to devote the coming year to science. There are rumors that the Babylonians are experimenting with a wheel that wields water, and my people could use such a contraption. For the Gods have revealed that a great drought will be upon us…

"Ur, 1830 BC" is a game about irrigation in Mesopotamia. It is based lightly on Francis Tresham's successful series of 18XX train games, of which 1830 is the most famous. As this series is the favorite non-Splotter game of three of Splotter's designers, we wanted to make a contribution to this great gaming tradition. So here it is!

### **1.2 Game Overview**

Each player controls a dynasty in ancient Mesopotamia. The game is played in rounds, each consisting of three phases:

- **• Settlement Phase.** Players buy and sell land, trying to settle the areas that will yield most. If the population grows large enough, states emerge.
- **• Development Phase.** States build the irrigation works (canals, reservoirs, and pumps)
- **• Rainy Season.** Water comes down the rivers and is diverted to be used for irrigation. Each plot of irrigated land yields income to the land owner and to the state that operates the waterworks.

As more and more states are founded, the technological development in waterworks and digging tools drives old equipment out of use. Canals are built faster and faster, land yield goes up, and more and more land becomes available for agriculture.

The board shows spaces with land (desert, forest, hills, and savannah) and rivers depicted on them. Each of these spaces is called an **area**. Groups of adjacent areas of the same land type are called **regions**. Additionally, there are thick colored outlines representing the **states**. All the areas within these outlines are part of the state.

The game involves three levels of strategy: first, smart buying and selling on the land market; second, tactical play developing the right land and canals on the map; and third, strategic anticipation and control of the speed of technological development.

### **1.3 A Note on Treasuries**

The notion of treasuries is vital to this game. There are two kinds of treasuries: private and state. Private treasuries are controlled by players. They are used to buy and sell land, and count towards victory in the end. State treasuries are controlled by monarchs. They can be used to buy waterworks and hire digging crews, and do not count towards victory. It is vital to keep the two separated. State treasuries must be placed on a state treasury chart. Private money must be placed on the table in front of the respective player.

All treasuries, state and private, may be counted by any player at any time.

Put the cards showing digging teams on the slots provided.

Use the independent nation cards to establish turn order. Use the number of cards corresponding to the number of players and deal one card to each player, face down. The initial letters on the cards indicate the order of play (A plays first, then B, et cetera). Players may have to change seats to ensure that play can proceed clockwise. The player who got card A gets the primogeniture card (this is the right-of-birth card, showing a mess of pottage). This player starts the game.

Take back the six independent nation cards and put them on the map in alphabetical order for use in the next part of the game.

## **3 Independent Nations**

### **3.1 Overview**

There are six independent nations: Ashur, Barahshum, Calah, Der, Eridu, and First Akkadians. Each nation occupies a piece of land on the board and has a card that details the nation's treaty price, the tribute that it pays its owner, and any special skills that the nation may accord its owner.

At any point during play, each nation is either owned by a player, **assimilated** (owned by a state), or **dissolved** (removed from the game). The nations are divided using the process described below. Later in the game, states may assimilate nations by buying them from their owners (either a player or another state). Some nations provide special skills that require dissolving them; later in the game, all the nations are dissolved.

### **3.2 Dividing The Independent Nations**

Players take turns in the division of the independent nations. The nations are acquired by players in alphabetical order. When a player takes a turn, the **treaty nation** is the first in order that has not yet been acquired (and is thus ready to be acquired for its treaty price). The player may do one of three things:

- Acquire the treaty nation for its treaty price (but see below if that nation already has an offer).
- Make an offer on another nation later in order.
- Pass (take no action).

To make an offer to a later nation, a player proceeds as follows:

- If there was no offer for that nation, the amount offered must be at least 5 SPL greater than the nation's treaty price. The amount offered and the player's ownership marker are placed below the nation's card.
- If there was already an offer for that nation, the amount offered must be at least 5 SPL greater than the previous offer. The previous offer (but not the ownership marker) is returned to the player who made that offer. The new offer and current player's ownership marker are placed to the right of the highest previous offer.

(In the unlikely event that the player had already made an offer for this nation, move their marker to the right and leave with it the amount of the latest offer. This might be done to change the bidding order in a subsequent auction or to discourage other players from making an offer.)

If there is already an offer for the first available nation when a player's turn would start, the player cannot choose to acquire the nation for its treaty price. Instead, the game is interrupted to allow that nation to be acquired (through the process described below) before the player can take their turn.

If only one player had made an offer on that nation, that player acquires the nation for the amount offered. If more than one player has made an offer, an auction results. The offer of the player whose ownership marker is farthest to the right is the initial bid. Bidding then proceeds among the players who made offers on the nation in the order determined by their ownership markers (from left to right and then back around). Each bid must be at least 5 SPL higher than the previous. A player may decline to bid, thus dropping out of the auction. When all but one of the bidding players have dropped out, the remaining player acquires the nation for the highest bid. Players retrieve their ownership markers.

The next nation now becomes the treaty nation unless it also has an offer, in which case the process just described repeats. Only when the next nation has no offer does it become the treaty nation and the original player takes a turn.

When a player acquires a nation (through any of the means described above), the player pays the price to the bank, except for First Akkadians; for that nation, the price is paid to the treasury of the state of Akkad.

A player acquiring First Akkadians places three of their ownership markers (face up) on the three Savannah lands that make up that nation. That player also becomes the Monarch of Akkad (rules about Monarchs are given with the settlement phase).

*For example, in a 4-player game: Player 1 buys Ashur at 20 SPL. Barahshum is now the treaty nation. Player 2 offers 115 SPL for Der and puts the money aside. Player 3 then offers 120 SPL for Der and puts it aside; player 2 regains use of their 115 SPL. Player 4 offers 175 SPL for Eridu. Player 1 buys Barahshum at 40 SPL; Calah becomes the treaty nation. Player 2 passes. Player 3 buys Calah; players 2 and 3 then bid for Der in an auction, which is won by player 2 at 150 SPL (player 3 gets back their offer of 120 SPL). Eridu then goes to player 4 for 175 SPL, as they were the only one to make an offer. Play proceeds with player 4, as player 3 was the last one to get a treaty nation. Player 4 passes. Player 1 then pays the treaty price of 220 SPL for the First Akkadians to join them.*

If all players pass consecutively before all nations are acquired, the following occurs:

- If Ashur is the treaty nation, reduce its price by 5 SPL. If all players again pass consecutively, the price is reduced by another 5 SPL. Once this has happened four times (reducing the price to 0 SPL), the current player acquires Ashur for 0 SPL and the turn passes to the next player.
- If Ashur is not the treaty nation, each player receives from the bank the tribute for each nation that they have already acquired. Play then continues normally (the price of the treaty nation does not change).

If it becomes a player's turn and all independent nations have been acquired (e.g., because there had been offers on all of them), that player takes the primogeniture card and the first round of play begins.

## **4 Settlement Phase**

### **4.1 Order Of Play**

In the settlement phase, players take turns to buy or sell land areas. The player holding the primogeniture card starts, and play proceeds clockwise. Players take turns in this phase as long as any player does something during their turn; the phase ends as soon as all players pass consecutively.

### **4.2 Land Areas and Prices**

A **land area** (or just "area") is an area of the map demarcated by thin black lines. There are four types of land indicated on the map: hills, forest, savannah, and desert.

There are also **river areas**, which are the blue areas with a river running through them. Although river areas flow through the states, the states have no entitlements. The river areas are effectively neutral.

Each type of land has a fixed **colonization price** as printed on the land price bar. (As noted in the table, the colonization price of cities differs from that of normal land.) The current **sale price**, which may be higher or lower than the colonization price, is indicated by a marker which moves on the land price bar.

| Type of land | Normal land | City land |
|--------------|-------------|-----------|
| Hills        | 100         | 100       |
| Forest       | 82          | 100       |
| Savannah     | 71          | 82        |
| Desert       | 60          | 71        |

Each area also belongs to a state, as

indicated by the colored boundaries. There are six states. A state is called **emerging** if fewer than six land areas in that state have been colonized. It becomes **active** at the end of a settlement phase if six or more land areas have been colonized by the end of that phase.

On their turn, a player may either:

- sell as much of their land as they want, and/or buy one area,
- bid for primogeniture,
- or pass.

### **4.3 Buying Land**

In a turn of a settlement phase, a player may buy one land area. They may do so before or after selling land. They may also sell, buy, and sell again during a turn. Players in order may take multiple turns within a settlement phase and may buy up to one land area per turn, but may sell multiple areas each turn.

To buy a land area that has never been bought or sold (such land will have no ownership marker on it), a player pays the fixed colonization price printed on the price bar into the treasury of the state in which the land lies.

To buy land that was bought and later sold (such land will have a flipped-over counter on it), a player pays to the bank the current sale price indicated by the marker on the price bar. (See 4.7, City Prices, for an exception.) The flipped-over counter is returned to its owner.

The buyer places one of their ownership markers on the land that they bought. If the buyer has no counters left to do so, they cannot buy the land. It is not allowed to take back flipped-over counters from sold land until that land is bought by another player.

A player may not buy:

- land that belongs to other players;
- river areas;
- land of a type that player has already sold during the current settlement phase;
- land in an independent nation that is owned by a player;
- land of any type in an emerging state whose land that player has already sold during the current settlement phase (see 4.8, Emerging States).

*Example: Player 1 sells a desert in Elam, which is still emerging as only four pieces of land have been sold so far. They also sell a desert in Babylon, which is already active. For the remainder of this phase, they may no longer buy any deserts, nor any land in Elam. They may, however, buy savannah or forest in Babylon (which was already active) or anywhere else. They may sell whatever they buy in the same turn, or later in the phase. Of course, they may also hang on to their investments.*

### **4.4 Selling Land**

On their turn, a player may sell land. To do so, they may perform one or more selling actions, in each action, selling one or more land areas of one type. A player may perform multiple selling actions for a particular type of land in one turn.

To sell land, a player flips over their ownership marker(s) on the land sold to indicate it is no longer theirs. In return, they get the price indicated by the current price marker of that type of land from the bank. If, in one selling action, a player sells more than one area, they get the indicated amount for each area before prices are changed.

After each selling action, the price of that land type drops one box per area sold, up to a maximum of three boxes. If the original land price was above the shaded area, it will not drop further than the first box of the shaded area. If the land price was already in the shaded area before the selling action, it drops by only one box per action, independent of the amount of land sold.

A player may not sell the last player-owned area in a state (whether active or emerging).

*Example: Player 1 wants to sell five hill areas currently valued at 100. On their turn, they may sell all five in one action, receiving 500 SPL and lowering the price by three steps to 76. Alternatively, they may sell three of the hill areas, receiving 300 SPL and lowering the price to 76, and then sell the remaining two, receiving an additional 152 SPL and lowering the price further to 67.*

### **4.5 Bidding For Primogeniture**

Th e primogeniture card gives a player the right to start the next settlement phase. A player may bid for primogeniture instead of buying and selling. Th e amount must be higher than the previous amount bid (if any). Aft er bidding, the player takes the primogeniture card and puts their bid on it, returning any previous bid to the appropriate player.

At the end of the settlement phase, the player with the primogeniture card takes their bid back (no money is paid to the bank) and keeps the card. If no player bids for primogeniture, the card remains with its current owner.

Th e owner of the primogeniture starts the settlement phase of the next round.

### **4.6 Pass**

A player may also pass. If all players pass consecutively, the settlement phase ends and the development phase begins. If, however, another player buys land, sells land, or bids for primogeniture, the passing player will get another turn.

### **4.7 City Prices**

Cities are indicated on the map with a Ziggurat (an ancient religious structure, resembling a pyramid). Cities yield more than ordinary land, but they are also more expensive. Th ey are priced equal to the next highest land price on the price bar (or the highest if there is none). So:

- Th e colonization prices follow the table in section 4.2, Land Areas and Prices, where a desert city is as expensive as savannah, a savannah city is as expensive as forest, and both forest and hill cities are valued as hills.
- Th e current sale price follows that of the next highest marker if there is one. Th e highest marker specifi es the price for normal land and city land of that type.

*Example (see picture): Th e desert marker is at 53; the savannah and hills markers are at 67; the forest marker is at 112. Desert cities are now worth 67 (next highest price). Savannah and hill cities are worth 112 (next highest marker), as are forest cities (no higher marker present).*

As with normal land, if a city land is sold, the price of that type of land drops. Th us, selling a desert city will decrease the price of deserts, but it might not decrease the price of desert cities. (Th e price of desert cities may be determined by the next highest marker.) Th e rules pertaining to buying and selling land types remain the same regardless of the presence of cities.

### **4.8 Emerging States**

Colonizing an area increases the population of the state that the area is in. At the start of the game, all states are **emerging**; they are minor entities with no centralized tax revenues.

A state becomes **active** if six pieces of its land have been colonized (i.e. there is a marker on it, either fl ipped or unfl ipped) at the end of a settlement phase. States do not become active during a settlement phase. Only active states participate in the development phase.

Once a state is active, it cannot become emerging again.

### **4.9 Monarchs**

States are controlled by monarchs who take all the actions and make decisions on behalf of the states. Th e fi rst player to acquire land in a state becomes its monarch. To indicate this, give the treasury chart of the state to the current monarch.

If a player buys land in a state so that they have more than the current monarch, they become the new monarch. Th e treasury should change accordingly.

If a monarch sells land in their state so that they own less than another player, they must hand the treasury chart to the player who now has the most land. If multiple such players tie, the tied player who is fi rst in clockwise order from the previous monarch becomes the new monarch.

## **5 Development Phase**

### **5.1 Order Of Play**

In the development phase, monarchs decide on actions of their states. Only active states get a turn.

Th e development phase consists of three parts.

- 1. All independent nations contribute income to their owner.
- 2. Th e player or state owning Eridu may dig a canal using a 2-digger (see the next section for details).
- 3. Active states may develop their land. Each state may do the following (in the order indicated):
  - I. build canals;
  - II. construct waterworks, hire digging crews, and assimilate independent nations.

Each active state takes a turn in a development phase.

#### **5.1.1 Independent Nations**

Each independent nation contributes the amount indicated on its card to its owner from the bank. If it is owned by a player, it pays to the owning player. If it is assimilated, it pays to the owning state's treasury. Th e money is paid from the bank. (No money is paid by dissolved nations.)

#### **5.1.2 States**

Th e order in which active states play depends on the amount of land owned by the players, that is, containing non-fl ipped markers (land that was sold but not yet bought again does not count here). Th e state of which the smallest amount of land is owned plays fi rst. In case of a tie between two or more states, their order is indicated on the playing board: the state with the lowest number plays fi rst. Note that emerging states do not play at all in the development phase.

Th e order in which active states play is determined at the start of the development phase and does not change during the phase, even if land may occasionally be sold during the phase. At the beginning of each development phase, place the states' hour-glass markers in the row at the top of the board to indicate the playing order.

### **5.2 Digging Canals**

To dig a canal, a state must have a digging crew. Since these can only be hired aft er digging canals, according to the order of play, a newly active state can never build canals in its fi rst turn.

Th e board already contains a few canals. More canals are dug by placing wood canal markers on the board to connect two areas. A canal may be dug from the center of any area already containing a river or canal to the center of any neighboring area. All canals and rivers in a particular area are always connected to each other. Canals can run in circles, or connect two rivers. Th ey cannot run off the board.

Th e number on a digging crew's card is the amount of digging points it may use each turn. Digging one canal costs one digging point. So, a 3-digging crew may dig three canals, connecting four areas. Th e stretch dug by a crew in a particular turn must form one line. A crew does not have to use all its digging points.

Th e stretch dug by each crew in a particular turn must form one sequence of areas called a **path**. An area may not be repeated in a path, and none of the areas in a path can belong to an independent nation owned by a player. If an independent nation has been assimilated by a state or dissolved, any crew may dig on its land. For the rest, crews may dig anywhere else on the board, even on land belonging to other players, and within the borders of other states.

*Existing canal*

*Digging example 1. A state has a 4-digging team. Digging the line is allowed. Digging the canals is not, as they do not run in one line.*

If a digging crew starts or ends on a river area, or on land already connected to two or more lands, the crew must build a junction point. Each junction costs one extra digging point. Junctions can only be made at the start or the end of the stretch dug by a crew in a particular turn. Junctions in the middle of a stretch are not allowed. Digging a canal which starts and ends on the same piece of land is not allowed, nor is it allowed to dig a canal which ends in a Y-junction with itself. One cannot make a junction without a canal; nor is it possible to combine two crews to let one make a junction and the other the canal.

If the path contains only two areas, they cannot both be river areas, and they cannot already be connected by a canal. If the path contains more than two areas, all areas besides the start and the end must be land areas without a canal.

A digging crew can build a canal on a path only if it has suffi cient digging points to do so:

- A 1+1 digging crew has 1 digging point that can be used for a canal, and 1 digging point that can be used for a junction.
- A 2/3/4 digging crew provides 2/3/4 digging points.

### **5.3 Constructing Waterworks and Hiring Digging Crews**

Apart from canals, a state can construct two types of waterworks: reservoirs and pumps. It can also hire digging crews. Th ese are represented by cards. Although a state can buy any number of cards and use them in any combination, it must always buy the most primitive cards available fi rst. Th us, all 2/1/1+1-cards must be sold before the 4/2/2-cards become available.

If a state buys a card, it must immediately decide whether to use it as a reservoir, a pump, or a digging crew.

#### **5.3.1 Constructing Waterworks**

Reservoirs and pumps are the two types of waterworks that can be constructed. Reservoirs are built on river areas and pumps are built on land areas. Th ey are represented by the same tokens, but the number on the token is interpreted diff erently: reservoirs can pump the *indicated amount* of water *one step* to a neighboring land, provided a canal is present. Pumps may distribute *any amount* of water up to the *indicated distance*, measured by the number of canals it is routed through.

If a state has no tokens left to represent a waterwork with the specifi ed number, it cannot buy such a waterwork.

Pumps and reservoirs, once built, cannot be moved or removed except as a consequence of storing a harvest (see 6.2.1, Storing or Distributing the Harvests). Th ey cannot be upgraded.

### **5.3.1.1 Reservoirs**

A reservoir can only be placed on river areas. It may even be placed on a river area within another state's territory. A river area cannot contain more than one reservoir. If the river area has a confl uence (where two rivers meet), the reservoir must be placed downstream of the confl uence.

Th e capacity of a reservoir is the large number indicated in the lower left corner of the card and the smaller number next to it indicates its price. Th e state pays the price of the reservoir from its treasury to the bank. Th e state's monarch then places a reservoir marker of the state's color and of the matching capacity on a river area. Aft er the marker has been placed, the card is put in the box.

#### **5.3.1.2 Pumps**

A pump can be placed on any land area that does not already contain one. In areas belonging to an independent nation, a pump can be placed only aft er that nation has been assimilated or has dissolved. If the area is owned by a player, that player may deny permission to build the pump on their land. Pumps can be built on areas containing canals and on areas that are still devoid of water. Th ey can also be built outside of the state's territory.

Th e reach of a pump is indicated by the large number in the upper left corner of the card and the small number next to it indicates its price. Th e state pays the price of the pump from its treasury to the bank. Th e state's monarch places a pump marker of the state's color and of the matching capacity on the area. Aft er the marker has been placed, the card is put in the box.

#### **5.3.2 Hiring Digging Crews**

If the state uses the card to hire a digging crew, the card is placed in the state's treasury. Th e digging crew's digging points (see 5.2, Digging Canals) is indicated by the large number in the top right corner of the card and the smaller number next to it indicates its price. Th e state pays the price of the digging crew from its treasury to the bank.

### **5.4 Dawn of a New Era**

When the fi rst card of a particular set is bought, a new era of technology starts. Th e amount of water available and the harvest per area depend on the current era.

As soon as the third era starts, all 1+1-digging crews leave; they are no longer willing to work with the old tools. Th e cards are placed in the box and will no longer be used. Th e state that has started the third era may immediately assimilate independent nations (see Assimilating Independent Nations).

At the start of the fourth era, all 2-digging crews leave. In addition, all independent nations dissolve forever. Th eir cards are put in the box.

At the start of the fi nal era (Millennium era), all 3-digging crews leave. Th eir cards are put in the box.

### **5.5 Canal Maintenance and Revolution**

An active state is required always to have a digging crew to perform maintenance on existing canals and river beds. Th is crew must be present at the end of a state's turn. A state which has just become active, or which has lost its digging crews at the dawn of a new era, has to hire a digging crew in its next turn.

If the state treasury does not suffi ce to hire a digging crew, the monarch must pay the diff erence from their personal treasury. If this is also not enough, they must immediately sell land until they have enough money to pay. If the monarch cannot raise the money, a revolution occurs. Th e monarch must then sell all the land they can, and forfeits all their money to the bank. Th e game ends aft er the next rainy season. During that rainy season, the state in which a revolution has occurred will function normally; its harvest will always be distributed. Th e former monarch gets their share of the harvest as usual.

If the monarch is forced to sell land, they may not sell land if that would cause someone else to become monarch of the revolting state. (It is allowed to sell land that would change the ownership of other states.) Although they may freely choose which lands to sell, they must stop selling as soon as they have raised enough money to hire the digging crew. Th e cash left aft er having hired the digging crew must be less than the price of the cheapest land sold. Th e monarch keeps this as personal money.

Th e monarch is allowed to use the state treasury to assimilate nations or buy waterworks fi rst, even if this forces them into an insolvent position they could otherwise have avoided. It is not allowed to use any personal money or money from land sold to buy waterworks or second digging crews, however.

Land prices are adjusted as normal (see 4.4, Selling Land). Although the monarch of the current state cannot change, the monarchy of other states may pass on to other players as a result of these sales. Th e order in which states play is not aff ected.

### **5.6 Assimilating Independent Nations**

An active state can assimilate independent nations by buying them from their owners.

Th e assimilating state and the current 'owner' of the nation must agree on a price that is at least half the nation's treaty price and at most twice that price. Th e assimilating state pays that price from its treasury to the 'owner' (to the owning player's private treasury or the owning state's treasury, as appropriate). Aft er a nation has been assimilated it cannot be given or sold back to a player.

Th is action may only be taken during the state's turn in the development phase aft er the dawn of the third era (that is, aft er the fi rst 3-card has been sold). As soon as an independent nation has been assimilated, all its land is open for buying, selling, digging, and building waterworks.

## **6 The Rainy Season**

### **6.1 Dividing The Water**

Finally, water fl ows down the rivers, starting at the sources. Th e main rivers are handled one by one in the order indicated on the map. Put a number of blue water markers at the source of each of the three rivers. Th e amount of water per source depends on the current era (see the drops on the board).

Start by moving the main water fl ow of river 1 downstream. At a river confl uence, the water from one tributary should wait until the other tributary has brought in its water.

If water reaches a waterwork, the monarch of the state that operates the waterwork must decide what to do with the water.

If the water reaches a reservoir, this reservoir must pump water equal to its capacity to a neighboring area connected by a direct canal. It may distribute the water among diff erent neighboring areas, as long as it is connected to each of them. A M(ill)-reservoir has unlimited capacity and must therefore distribute all water it gets.

If the water reaches a pump, this pump can distribute the water over the indicated distance measured along canals, but not past other pumps. A pump can distribute any amount of water. It can also pump water to another pump within reach, which may then pump the water even further. A Mill (M-pump) can distribute water over any distance, but still it cannot pump past other pumps.

Land is irrigated by putting a water marker on it. An area can be irrigated only if it is owned by a player. Each land can only be irrigated once. To irrigate land, a state must be able to reach it using reservoirs and/or pumps. Th e monarch owning the waterwork puts one water marker on the area.

A state may choose which lands to irrigate. However, the monarch controlling a pump is obliged to get rid of as much water as possible: they must either irrigate as much land as they can or pump the water to a pump, of any state, where it has not yet been. In addition, a pump must always irrigate the area it is on if the area is owned by a player. If a pump brings water to an area containing another pump, the latter will irrigate the area (if it is owned by a player).

Note that it is possible to pump water across an area without irrigating it. For example, if there is a canal from area A to area B and from area B to area C, with a 2-pump on area A, then the pump can bring water to area C without irrigating area B. Area B does not need to be owned by a player, but to irrigate area B it must have an owner.

If a pump has excess water (because there was no more land left to irrigate), the water returns to the previous pump, which must either use it to irrigate land or send the water to a pump where it has not yet been.

### **6.2 Harvests**

Owners of irrigated areas get 5 SPL per irrigated area, or 10 SPL for a city.

A state irrigates an area if it owns the fi nal pump or reservoir in the series that has brought water to that area. If an area contains a pump, the state that owns that pump is said to irrigate the area.

A state harvests a certain amount of food for each area it irrigates. Th e amount varies between 20 and 30 SPL, depending on the current era (see the numbers on the squares under the stacks of cards on the game board). Th e amount is doubled for cities.

### **6.2.1 Storing or Distributing the Harvests**

Round down before multiplying and put any remaining money in the state treasury. Exception: if only one player has land in the state, they get the entire harvest. Note that ownership of an independent nation does not qualify as ownership of that nation's land area(s).

## **6.3 Effect Of Irrigation On Land Prices**

All types of land increase in value by a number of steps equal to the number of regions of that type that were irrigated this turn. A region is a continuous group of land areas of the same type. Th ere are thus 5 regions of hills, 4 of desert and forest, and 3 of savannah.

Aft er the prices have been adjusted, remove the water markers from the board, and play proceeds with the settlement phase of the next round; the player who has the primogeniture card starts.

# **7 Game End**

Th e game ends at the end of the rainy season, if:

- a revolution has occurred because a monarch cannot maintain the waterworks, or
- an invasion of the Southern Peoples occurs because no water fl ows off the board from the Southernmost river area, meaning all water was used for irrigation.

When the game ends, the player with the most valuable set of assets wins the game. Only private money and land value count for a player's assets. State treasuries and independent nations are not counted.

## **9 List of Independent Nations**

### **9.1 Ashur**

Price: 20 SPL

Yield per round: 10 SPL

Special properties: none. Cannot be dissolved by a state, but can be assimilated.

### **9.2 Barahshum**

Price: 40 SPL

Yield per round: 20 SPL

Special properties: The owning player or state may dissolve the nation to dig a canal from the Barahshum homeland to any adjacent area; in doing so, it may create one or two junctions. A player may dissolve Barahshum at any time during the settlement phase, or in between states in the development phase. This may be used in the same way by a state after assimilation, but only in the state's turn in the development phase.

### **9.3 Calah**

Price: 70 SPL

Yield per round: 25 SPL

Special properties: The owning state may dissolve Calah for a free waterwork of the current type in the hills of Calah or any areas adjacent to Calah during its turn. Normal rules apply (waterwork card discarded; land owner must give permission; no more than one waterwork per land).

### **9.4 Der**

Price: 110 SPL

Yield per round: 30 SPL

Special properties: May be dissolved by a player to buy one available forest land at no cost during the player's turn in the settlement phase. This counts as the turn's buy action. No money goes into the state treasury. The state is not compensated in any way. The land may be a normal or city forest area. Cannot be dissolved by a state, but can be assimilated.

### **9.5 Eridu**

Price: 160 SPL

Yield per round: 35 SPL

Special properties: Eridu can be used to dig canals before the states operate. Eridu acts as a 2-digging crew in all respects and is controlled by the owning player or the monarch of the controlling state. Each round, it may dig before any of the state diggers, even if it is owned by a state. Using this special property does not cause Eridu to be dissolved.

A state owning Eridu need not have another digging crew. If a player owns Eridu, it does not relieve any of the states for which that player is monarch of the obligation to have a digging crew.

### **9.6 First Akkadians**

Price: 220 SPL

Yield per round: 35 SPL

Special properties: The money paid for this nation goes into the state of Akkad. The player who bought the First Akkadians immediately gets three Savannah lands in the state of Akkad. Unlike other independent nations, these fields can be owned by a player while the First Akkadians is an independent nation owned by a player.

This independent nation dissolves as soon as the state of Akkad hires a digging crew (assimilating Eridu does not count as hiring a digging crew).

The First Akkadians cannot be assimilated.
